## Option B, the number-led one

For the frame that leads with 46 percent. Shorter, harder, and the control number
does the honesty work inside the caption instead of the image.

> Twenty years fallow. One season of biology. 46 percent more organic matter.
>
> Sandra Niggemeyer put biologically complete compost into one plot of Texas clay and sand, teas and extracts into a second, and water only into a third. Bacteria and fungi produce the glues that bind soil particles into aggregates. Fungal hyphae weave those into larger ones. Protozoa and nematodes graze the bacteria and release nutrients at the root, in the form the plant can use.

## The LinkedIn adaptation

Same facts, prose, no hashtag block. About 15 minutes from any option above.

> A field trial worth reading, because of what it did not show.
>
> Sandra Niggemeyer completed her field trial this season in Van, Texas: butternut squash on clay and sand that had been fallow for twenty years. Three plots on the same ground. Biologically complete compost tilled into the first. Teas and extracts applied through the season on the second. Water only on the third, as the control.
>
> Organic matter gained over the season: 46 percent on the compost plot, 14 percent on the control, 1 percent on the tea plot.
>
> Harvest: 35 ounces, 30 ounces and 19 ounces respectively. That is roughly two pounds against one. A real difference, and a small enough sample that nobody should scale it.
>
> The result that does not fit the story is the interesting one. The tea plot gained almost no organic matter and carried the fewest squash bugs. The control carried the most. Suppressing pests and building soil structure are different mechanisms, and a single unreplicated season on one field cannot separate them.
>
> The season was also against her. Hail killed the first planting. Rodents took the second. Johnson grass ran the beds by June.
>
> Her conclusion: soil biology rebuilds the ground before it rebuilds the yield, and in degraded soil the first season is the ground.
>
> We publish these as we get them, including the ones that disappoint. If you are running trials on degraded ground, what did your first season give you?

4 and 5 believable.

## What makes one of these get saved

Worth knowing before choosing, because saves are the metric the monthly review
actually uses.

- **A number a reader can repeat.** 46 against 14 is repeatable. "Significant
  improvement" is not.
- **A result that contradicts the expected story.** The tea plot is the whole
  reason option A is the recommended one.
- **Something reusable.** The three-plot design in slide 3 is a template a
  grower can copy on their own ground. That is the part people screenshot.
- **Not a congratulation.** A graduation notice gets a like. A trial result gets
  a save.
